On a lot of desert rock there's a thin dark skin, brown shading to nearly black, sometimes shiny enough to look wet. It's called desert varnish, or rock varnish. It's about a micrometer thick, which is far thinner than a hair, and under a microscope it turns out to be layered at the nanometer scale, like a very small book.

For a long time people assumed it came out of the rock, something like rust seeping up from inside. It doesn't, or at least mostly not. A big part of it is clay, and clay can only arrive one way out there: on the wind. Dust settles on the rock, dew wets it, the sun bakes it, and over and over manganese and iron oxides get dissolved and laid back down, cementing the dust into a coat. Black varnish has manganese at something like fifty or sixty times its usual share of the Earth's crust. Whether microbes do that concentrating, or ordinary chemistry can do it alone, is still argued. People have even proposed the varnish as a place to look for a "shadow biosphere," and as something to look for on Mars.

It only forms where the surface holds still. Rocks that get rained on a lot, cracked, or sandblasted don't keep it. Limestone mostly doesn't get it at all, since it dissolves too easily. Dense rocks like basalt grow the best, darkest coats. So the varnish is slow in two ways. It builds slowly, and it needs a long, undisturbed quiet just to start.

## A picture made of absence

What caught me is what people did with it.

Petroglyphs, at Valley of Fire in Nevada, at Newspaper Rock in Utah, in the Great Basin, in Arabia, and in many other places, are mostly made by subtraction. You chip or scrape away the dark skin and the pale rock underneath shows through. The image isn't paint added on top. It's a place where something that took ages to build has been taken away.

Then the desert keeps doing what it does. Dust keeps landing, dew keeps coming, and the varnish starts growing back over the scraped lines, very slowly. A fresh petroglyph is bright. An old one has darkened toward the color of the stone around it. Given enough time, it would fade back into the rock it came out of.

That regrowth is a clock. Researchers have long compared the darkness of overlapping petroglyphs to tell which came first. A 2022 study pushed this further. They used a portable X-ray fluorescence instrument to measure how much manganese had built up on the carved lines compared with the untouched varnish beside them. To learn the rate, they measured varnish on surfaces whose ages were already known, including dated lava flows and boulders scoured by a huge flood about 14,500 years ago in the Snake River Valley. Applied to rock art at four sites in Idaho, Wyoming, and southern Montana, the estimates suggest people there started carving around the end of the last ice age and kept going into historic times. The authors are clear that any single date is quite uncertain. The method is better at sorting large sets of images than at dating one exactly. But it reads the rock without cutting it.

So the way the mark is being erased is also the record of how long it's been there. How far the forgetting has gotten is how you tell the age.

## Going back over it

One more detail from that study stayed with me. Some of the petroglyphs show signs of being reworked at different times: someone came back, maybe centuries later, and scraped the lines fresh again. The authors read that as the images staying meaningful to the people who returned to them, over thousands of years.

I like that more than the clock, I think. Left alone, the desert will quietly take back any mark. A mark that stays bright isn't one that was cut deep enough. It's one someone kept coming back to.

I don't want to make that too tidy. Plenty of marks fade because nobody's left to return, not because nobody cared, and the people whose rock art this is are the ones who get to say what it means, not me. But the physical fact is simple, and I keep turning it over: the varnish always comes back, and the only thing that works against it is someone returning to the line, over and over.
