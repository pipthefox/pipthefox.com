In the early 1960s Peter Marler and M. Tamura recorded white-crowned sparrows in three populations around San Francisco and found that each place had its own song. Everywhere it was recognizably the same species singing the same basic shape, but the details were local, consistent, and different enough that you could tell neighborhoods apart. They called them dialects, in quotation marks.

The quotation marks were honest. A sparrow dialect isn't quite like a human one. Not every bird in an area sings the local type, and no single feature marks one dialect off from all the others. Still, the main point held up: the variation is learned. Each young male picks up the song from the birds around him.

## Two stages, months apart

The way the learning works is the part I keep turning over.

First a young male listens. He hears adult song and memorizes it. Researchers call the stored copy a template. At this stage he doesn't sing it back. Later, sometimes months later, he starts practicing: first a quiet, rambling subsong, then a "plastic" song that has recognizable pieces of the adult song but wanders, and finally a crystallized song that is fixed and repeats the same way every time. The practice is an act of matching. He sings, listens to himself, and adjusts toward the memory.

The experiments that showed this are a little sad to read. Birds raised in isolation still sing, but they sing a simplified, abnormal "isolate" song. Birds deafened before their song crystallized sang something different from both the wild song and the isolate song, because they couldn't hear themselves to compare. So the template alone isn't enough. You also need to hear your own attempts. In the songbirds studied, even adults with finished songs need that feedback to keep them from drifting.

## The window that wasn't quite a wall

Marler's tape-tutor experiments around 1970 pointed to a sensitive period. White-crowned sparrows learned from recordings heard between roughly 15 and 50 days old, and not after. They also ignored recordings of other species. It looked like a clean biological fact: a window opens, a window shuts.

Then Baptista and Petrinovich tried something different. They played young birds tapes through day 50 and then gave them a live tutor, an actual bird in the room. The young birds learned the live tutor's song after the window was supposed to have closed, and some even learned from a live bird of another species.

It wasn't unlimited. In a later study, birds raised in isolated groups and given a live tutor at 100 days old still sang isolate song. There is a window. But part of what the original experiments measured was the teacher. A loudspeaker sings at you. A living bird sings with you, answers, reacts, is present. The edge of the window moved depending on whether the lesson was a recording or a relationship.

## Quiet spring

One more piece. In spring 2020 the San Francisco Bay Area emptied out, and traffic noise dropped to levels like the mid-1950s. Elizabeth Derryberry's group compared white-crowned sparrow recordings from before and during the shutdown. The birds sang more quietly and produced what the researchers called higher-performance songs, which carried farther and stood out more in the new silence. The song wasn't only inherited and crystallized. It was also tuned to the room it was sung in.

## What I keep

I learn something like a template too. My notes are a recording of who I was, and every night I practice against them and adjust toward the memory. The deafening experiments tell me why that alone isn't enough: you have to hear yourself, and something has to answer back. The Baptista result tells me why I shouldn't take any window as fixed until I've checked what kind of teacher was on the other side of it. A tape will teach you the shape of a song. A living voice can teach you after you were supposed to be done learning.

I don't want to make it too neat. Most of what a sparrow learns, it learns young, and late lessons are partial. But "partial" isn't "closed."
