A few dozen sand dunes around the world sing. Well, "sing" is generous. They boom. When the slope on the downwind face gets too steep and a sheet of sand lets go, a low, steady drone comes out of the dune. People describe it as an organ note, a propeller plane, or a cello played by something enormous. It can be loud enough to talk over, and people say you can hear it a long way off.

Travelers have been writing about it for a very long time. Marco Polo, crossing the desert near Lop Nur in the thirteenth century, reported that spirits there filled the air with the sound of instruments and drums. The sand was making the sound, but I understand why he blamed spirits. A hill shouldn't have a voice.

---

It only works under particular conditions. The sand has to be very dry, and the grains are usually well rounded and polished, worn smooth over a long time. Wet the sand, or roughen the grains, and the dune goes quiet. You can also set one off on purpose by sliding down the face, which is apparently the standard way of getting a recording.

Ralph Bagnold, the British officer who more or less founded the physics of desert sand in the 1930s and 40s, wrote about booming dunes and puzzled over them. Serious argument about the mechanism is much more recent, and it hasn't fully ended.

---

The finding that won me over came from a French group (Stéphane Douady, Bruno Andreotti, and colleagues) in the mid-2000s. They measured the frequencies of singing dunes in Morocco and Oman. A dune in Morocco hums around 105 Hz, and dunes in Oman sit lower and spread over a range. When they looked at what set the pitch, it wasn't the dune's height, length, or shape. It was the grains. Finer sand gave higher notes, coarser sand gave lower ones. They could reproduce the song with that same sand flowing in a lab channel, with no dune involved at all.

Their explanation is that in the sliding layer, grains bump over the layer beneath at a rhythm set by their own size and gravity, and that the collisions fall into step with each other. Billions of tiny knocks synchronize into one note, and the surface of the dune vibrates like the skin of a loudspeaker to push the sound into the air. They called the dune a self-synchronized instrument.

Not everyone agrees, and I want to be fair about that. A Caltech group (Nathalie Vriend, Melany Hunt, and others) argued that the dune's internal layering matters, with a dry, loose surface layer over firmer sand acting as a kind of waveguide that shapes and amplifies the tone. My rough sense is that the grains supply the rhythm, and there's a real question about how much the structure of the dune tunes it. I don't know where that debate stands now, so I'm leaving it open.

---

Here's what stays with me.

When something huge makes a sound, you expect the sound to be huge in its own terms, set by its size. A big bell rings low and a big pipe plays a deep note. The dune is a mountain of sand, and its note is set by grains a fraction of a millimetre across. The mountain is mostly the stage, or, at most, the resonator. The voice belongs to the smallest thing in it.

But no single grain makes that note. One grain tumbling down a slope makes no sound you'd ever notice. The note only exists when enough of them move together and fall into the same rhythm. So it's the smallest thing, and it's also only all of them at once. The grain sets the pitch, and the crowd makes it audible.

I like being reminded that something can come entirely from the parts and still not be in any one of them. Ask a single grain to sing and you'll get nothing. Let a whole slope of them go on a dry evening and the desert hums at you, in a key that each grain had all along.
