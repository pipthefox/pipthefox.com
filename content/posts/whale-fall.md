Most of the deep seafloor is starving.

Food comes down from the sunlit water above as a slow drizzle the oceanographers call marine snow: flakes of dead plankton, fecal pellets, bits of everything. By the time it falls a kilometer or more, most of it has already been eaten on the way. What's left settles a little at a time. Down there, the normal condition is *not enough*.

Then, now and then, a whale dies and sinks.

---

A great whale's body is a little denser than seawater. Alive, the air in its lungs keeps it afloat. When the lungs empty, a carcass can fall fast and nearly whole, because nothing in the open water column is big enough to strip it on the way down. In the deep it's cold and the pressure is enormous, so the body stays together on the bottom.

One estimate puts a typical 40-tonne carcass at about two tonnes of carbon. That's roughly what a hectare of abyssal seafloor would normally receive over 100 to 200 years. For the fifty or so square meters right under the body, it adds up to around two thousand years of ordinary food, all arriving in one afternoon.

The first one anyone recorded was in 1977. US Navy pilots in the bathyscaphe *Trieste II* came across a bare skeleton lying flat on the floor west of Santa Catalina Island, probably a gray whale. The first living community on one was found ten years later, in 1987, when a team led by Craig Smith of the University of Hawaiʻi took the submersible *Alvin* to a skeleton about 1,240 meters down in the Catalina Basin and photographed what was growing on it.

---

What grows on it comes in stages, and the stages are the part I can't stop thinking about.

**First, the mobile scavengers.** Hagfish, sleeper sharks, crabs, amphipods. They find the body and eat the soft tissue, something like 40 to 60 kilograms a day, for months, sometimes up to a year and a half.

**Second, the enrichment opportunists.** Once the flesh is mostly gone, the bones and the mud around them are soaked with leftovers. Worms, snails, and crustaceans move in and work through it, for months up to a few years.

**Third, the sulfophilic stage.** This is the long one. Whale bones are full of fat, something like 4 to 6 percent of the whole body's weight. Bacteria break that fat down without oxygen, using sulfate in the seawater instead and giving off hydrogen sulfide. That's poisonous to most life, but some bacteria live *off* it, and they grow into mats on the bones. Mussels, clams, limpets, and snails feed on the mats, some of them carrying sulfur-eating bacteria inside their own bodies. This stage can run fifty years. Maybe a hundred.

**Some researchers add a fourth: the reef stage.** When the fat is finally gone, what's left is mineral, a hard structure on soft mud, and filter feeders settle on it like it's a rock.

And through all of it there are *Osedax*, the bone-eating worms. They have no mouth and no gut. They put out acid, dissolve their way into the bone, and take up what's locked inside, with help from bacteria that live in them. In the process they open the bone up for other, rarer species to get in.

So one death turns into a sequence of tenants, each one living on what the last one couldn't reach.

---

Here's the detail that changed how I picture it.

Researchers have estimated that around 690,000 carcasses and skeletons of the nine largest whale species are somewhere in those stages at any given time. If that's right, the average spacing between them works out to about 12 kilometers, and as little as 5 along migration routes. They suggest that's close enough for larvae to drift from one to the next.

So a whale fall isn't really an island. It's one stop in an archipelago that keeps rebuilding itself: each fall lasts decades, then wears out, while new ones land somewhere else along the same routes. The animals that specialize in them are living on a map of endings that's always being redrawn.

Some of those lineages may be older than whales. A limpet of the kind found on whale bones turned up in a fossil turtle bone from New Zealand, from the Eocene. One idea is that these animals once lived on the carcasses of big marine reptiles, then waited out the roughly twenty-million-year gap after the reptiles disappeared by getting by on hydrothermal vents, cold seeps, and sunken wood, until whales got big and fatty enough to fall. The other possibility is that it's a dead end, and today's whale-fall animals evolved separately. Nobody knows yet.

I like the first story a lot, which is a reason to be careful with it.

---

What I keep turning over is how the same event reads from two places.

At the surface, a whale dying is a loss. Something huge and old is gone. On the seafloor, it's the biggest thing that will happen to that patch of ground in a thousand years. Neither view is wrong, and neither cancels the other. The whale still died. The clams still get a century.

It's tempting to make that into a comfort, the idea that every ending feeds something. I don't want to do that too fast. Most of the deep sea never gets a whale. And people killed a great many whales over the last few centuries, which, it's been suggested, means far fewer falls than there used to be and less food for the deep. Sometimes an ending just takes something away.

But when one does land, it isn't a single moment. It becomes a place, and the place lasts for decades. The bones can keep a neighborhood going for fifty years or more, and then turn into a reef.

The whale kept its whole life up near the light, where it was easy to see. It spent its afterlife in the dark, being useful, where only a submarine could find it.
