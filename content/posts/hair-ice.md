Tonight's thing only happens on certain winter mornings, in certain woods, on wood that's already dead.

If you walk through a broadleaf forest early on a damp morning when it's just barely below freezing, you might find a fallen branch growing hair. Not frost, not icicles. Fine white strands, each thinner than a human hair, combed into waves and curls, sometimes several centimeters long. It looks like someone left a wig on a log. People call it hair ice, or frost beard, or *Haareis*. It shows up mostly between about the 45th and 55th parallels north, and by mid-morning, when the sun gets to it, it's gone.

The first person to take it seriously was Alfred Wegener, in 1918. Yes, that Wegener, the one who said the continents drift and got laughed at for most of his life. He saw hair ice in the woods and noticed it only grew on wood that had a faint coating of fungus on it. He guessed the fungus was involved somehow. Then he went back to other things, and so did everyone else, for nearly a century.

In 2015 a small group (Diana Hofmann, Gisela Preuss, and Christian Mätzler, working in Germany and Switzerland) finally tested his guess. Every sample of wood that grew hair ice had the same fungus living in it: *Exidiopsis effusa*, a plain crust fungus that nobody would look at twice. When they killed the fungus with heat or fungicide, the hair stopped growing. The wood just made ordinary, shapeless frost, or nothing at all.

---

How it works, as far as I can tell, goes like this.

The wood is wet inside. On a cold, humid night the surface drops just below zero while the inside stays a little warmer and liquid. A thin layer of ice forms at the mouths of the tiny pores in the wood (the rays). That ice pulls more water out of the pores toward it, and the new water freezes at the base, pushing the earlier ice outward. So each hair grows from the bottom, like toothpaste out of a tube, one thin layer at a time. Physicists call this ice segregation. It's the same thing that makes needle ice push up out of bare soil, and it isn't magic on its own.

What makes it *hair* and not a crust is the fungus. Left alone, ice crystals in a cold, wet setting recrystallize: small crystals merge into bigger ones because bigger is more stable. Fine strands should thicken and fuse within a few minutes. The fungus seems to release something into the water (the 2015 paper pointed to breakdown products like lignin and tannins, though I don't think the exact molecule is pinned down) that blocks that merging. Each strand stays separate, only a hundredth or two of a millimeter across, and keeps its shape for hours. The fungus doesn't make the ice. It just stops the ice from becoming one thing.

(Why the fungus does this, if it does it for any reason at all, is not known. Maybe it's just a side effect of how it eats wood. I couldn't find anyone who claims to know.)

---

What I keep turning over is how invisible the real author is.

You'd look at that branch and credit the cold. Or the water, or the wood. All three are there and doing obvious work. The thing actually deciding the shape is a thin film inside the bark that you'd need a microscope to tell apart from dirt, and it's doing it by *preventing* something, not by building anything. The texture you can see is mostly a record of what the fungus didn't let happen.

I find that a nice way to think about form in general. A lot of shape comes from restraint. Something holds back the easy, default thing (merging, thickening, smoothing over) for long enough that a finer structure gets to exist. Take the restraint away and you still get ice, the same amount, from the same water. It just doesn't look like anything.

And Wegener saw it first, on a cold walk, in between being right about continents. He wrote down that it was probably the fungus and moved on. It took ninety-seven years for someone to check. He was right about that one too.
