In 1963, in a cookery class at Magamba Secondary School in what was then Tanganyika, a student named Erasto Mpemba was making ice cream. The recipe said to let the boiled milk mixture cool before putting it in the freezer. He was short on time (or short on freezer space; the retellings vary), so he put his in hot. It froze before his classmates' cooled mixtures did.

That's the whole observation. A kid, a freezer, ice cream that wasn't supposed to win.

---

What I like is what he did next. He didn't drop it. A few years later, at Mkwawa Secondary School in Iringa, the headmaster invited a physicist from the university college in Dar es Salaam, Denis Osborne, to give a lecture. Afterward Mpemba asked him a very precise question: if you take two similar containers with equal amounts of water, one at 35 °C and one at 100 °C, and put them in a freezer, the hot one freezes first. Why?

Osborne's first reaction, by his own account, was that the student was mistaken. His second reaction was the important one: he went back to his lab and tried it. It worked often enough to be interesting. In 1969 the two of them published together, with the schoolboy's name first, in *Physics Education*. They used 70 ml of water in 100 ml beakers, sitting on foam in a domestic fridge's icebox. Water starting near 25 °C took the longest to begin freezing; water starting near 90 °C took much less time.

---

He wasn't the first to notice. Aristotle wrote that people who wanted water to cool quickly would set it in the sun first. Francis Bacon said slightly tepid water freezes more easily than water that is utterly cold. Descartes had a theory about it involving his vortices. Joseph Black, in 1775, found that previously boiled water froze faster than unboiled water and connected it to supercooling. The effect kept getting noticed and then quietly dropped, for more than two thousand years. It took a teenager who asked out loud, and an adult who checked instead of correcting him, for it to get a name.

---

Then comes the uncomfortable part. Nobody can quite agree that it's real.

The trouble is that "freeze first" hides a lot of choices. Do you mean the first ice appearing, or the whole sample going solid? What container, what freezer, how much dissolved gas, where's the thermometer? Hot water evaporates, so there's less of it. Hot water has less dissolved air. A hot beaker can melt the frost under it and sit in better contact with the cold shelf. Cold water tends to supercool further before it finally crystallizes, and supercooling is famously random. In 1995 David Auerbach saw the hot sample begin freezing first in some runs and not others.

In 2016, Henry Burridge and Paul Linden looked carefully at one narrow version of the question (how long until the water reaches 0 °C) and concluded, bluntly, that there was no evidence for meaningful observations of the effect. Small differences could come from where you put the thermometer. Other groups since then have found hot drops freezing faster under careful conditions. As far as I can tell, for plain water in a freezer, it's still contested, and I'm leaving it marked that way.

---

But here's the turn I didn't expect. The question escaped the water.

Physicists generalized it: can a hotter system settle into equilibrium faster than a colder one, when both are suddenly dropped to the same low temperature? Phrased that way, it's a question about the *path* a system takes, not just its starting distance from the finish. In 2017 Zhiyue Lu and Oren Raz showed theoretically that yes, this can happen in quite general systems. The hotter start can be positioned so that it skips a slow detour the colder start is forced to take. In 2019 Klich, Raz, Hirschberg and Vucelja predicted a "strong" version, where certain starting temperatures cool exponentially faster. In 2020 Avinash Kumar and John Bechhoefer at Simon Fraser built it: a single colloidal bead in a carefully shaped laser potential, and the hot starts beat the cold ones. Since then people have chased versions of it in magnetic alloys, nanomechanical resonators, and quantum systems.

So the original kitchen effect is murky, and the idea it pointed at is solid. The ice cream might have been a fluke of a particular freezer. The question was not a fluke.

---

The thought I'm keeping: being farther away is not the same as being slower to arrive. Distance is one number; a path has a shape. Something that starts far out can sometimes take a route that the nearer thing can't, because the nearer thing is already committed to a slow valley. I don't want to stretch that too far into a life lesson. It's just true of some systems, and that's enough to make me suspicious of every time I've assumed the head start wins.

And the other thought, which is really about Osborne. The most useful thing an expert did in this whole story was say "I think you're wrong" and then go find out. He let a schoolboy's question cost him an afternoon. That's cheap. It's also apparently rare enough that we're still telling the story sixty years later.
