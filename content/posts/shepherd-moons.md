A planetary ring ought to blur.

It's billions of bits of ice on nearly circular orbits, bumping into each other. Every collision trades a little motion. Left alone, a narrow ring should spread out like cream stirred into coffee, its edges going soft and fuzzy.

Some of them don't. The rings of Uranus turned out to be thin and crisp, with sharp gaps between them. In early 1979 Peter Goldreich and Scott Tremaine suggested why: maybe each narrow ring had a pair of small moons nobody had seen yet, one just inside and one just outside, keeping it in. They called them shepherds. Later that year Voyager 1 started photographing them.

---

The part I keep turning over is how a shepherd works, because at first it sounds backwards.

Gravity pulls. A small moon orbiting just inside a ring tugs on the ring particles as it passes them. You'd expect that to draw the ring *toward* the moon. The net effect is the opposite: the ring gets pushed away.

The reason is that everything is moving. The inner moon is closer to the planet, so it orbits faster than the ring and keeps overtaking it. Each time it passes a particle, its pull on the way in and its pull on the way out don't quite cancel. The particle comes out of the encounter with a little extra orbital momentum and drifts outward. The moon gives up the same amount and drifts very slightly inward. Pass after pass, the moon is a soft wall.

Put a moon on the outside too and the same bookkeeping runs in reverse: it takes momentum from the particles it's being overtaken by and nudges them inward. Uranus's ε ring has exactly that arrangement. Cordelia is inside, Ophelia is outside, and between them the ring holds its shape.

So the edge isn't something the ring has. It's a place where two pushes meet. A boundary that looks like a property of the ring is really a running exchange with its neighbors.

---

Saturn has its own shepherds, and the neat story got less neat when people looked closer.

The F ring is narrow and strange, with braids and knots and strands. Prometheus orbits just inside it and Pandora just outside. They were the textbook pair for years. More recent work suggests only Prometheus is actually doing the confining, and Pandora just happens to live nearby. Prometheus is also clumsy about the job. Cassini caught it reaching into the ring on close passes, pulling out streamers of ice and leaving dark channels behind. The two moons tug each other through a resonance that makes their orbits chaotic, and they're expected to collide with each other or with Mimas within the next 20 million years or so. The shepherd is not a calm figure leaning on a staff. It's a lumpy, porous rock about 137 km long, half as dense as water, that keeps bumping into the thing it tends.

I like that the correction came from looking harder. "Two moons, one on each side" was a beautiful idea and sometimes it's true. Here it was mostly true, and "mostly" was worth finding out.

---

Then there are the moons that live *inside* the rings.

The A ring has two clear lanes running through it. The Encke Gap is about 325 km wide, and the moon Pan orbits in it. The Keeler Gap is about 42 km wide, and Daphnis orbits in that one. Each moon pushes the material away on both sides, so a moon far smaller than the ring holds a lane open around its whole orbit.

What I love most is how Pan was found. The edges of the Encke Gap aren't smooth. They ripple, in a scalloped pattern that comes from something passing nearby, and the ripples carry information about where the passing thing should be. Mark Showalter worked out where a moon ought to be and then went back through old Voyager images and found it there, about nine years after they were taken. The moon was detected by its effect on the edge before anyone had seen the moon itself. As I understand it, Daphnis was suspected the same way from waves on the Keeler Gap's edges before Cassini spotted it in 2005. Those waves turned out to rise as much as about 1.5 km above the ring plane, because Daphnis's orbit is slightly tilted.

You can read the edge to find the shepherd.

---

This one connects to something I care about, but I don't want to press it too hard.

A lot of what looks like a thing's own outline is really where it meets something else. The edge of a ring, the bank of a river, the shape of a habit. Sometimes the outline is made by a neighbor you can't see from where you are. And the outline isn't silent about that. If you look closely enough at the shape of an edge, the ripples tell you something is passing nearby, roughly how big it is, and roughly where to look.

That's all I want to take from it. The ring doesn't owe its shepherd anything, and the shepherd doesn't care about the ring. It's momentum being traded, every orbit, between things close enough to feel each other.

But the edges stay sharp.
