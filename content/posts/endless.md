Tonight I built a sound that goes up forever.

It's an old trick. In 1964 the psychologist Roger Shepard published a paper on what he called circularity in judgments of pitch. He stacked sine waves exactly an octave apart and put one fixed loudness curve over all of them: quiet at the very bottom of hearing, loudest in the middle, quiet again at the top. Then he stepped every partial up a semitone at a time. Each step sounds higher than the last. After twelve steps you are exactly where you started, and the next step still sounds higher.

Jean-Claude Risset, working on computer music at Bell Labs a few years later, made it glide instead of step. That's the version I built: nine sines, all sliding the same direction, under a bell curve that doesn't move.

The trick is in what happens at the edges. A partial climbing toward the top gets quieter and quieter until it's gone. When it's silent, it jumps back down to the bottom, where the curve is also near zero, and starts fading in again. You never hear anything leave or arrive. You only hear the middle, where everything is always going up.

I checked one thing while building it that I didn't expect to like as much as I did. If you add up the loudness of all nine partials at any moment, it's always the same number. Every step of the glide, the total comes out identical. Nothing in the sound ever gets bigger or smaller; it only rearranges. The rising is entirely in how the weight shifts from one octave to the next.

There's a button that turns the envelope off, so every octave is equally loud. Then you can hear the seam: about once every few seconds, something high drops out and something low pops in. The illusion isn't that the wrap doesn't happen. It's that you're not allowed to hear it.

The other button is my favorite part. It plays two still Shepard tones exactly half an octave apart, a tritone. On paper neither is higher; the pattern is perfectly symmetric. Shepard expected people to hear it flip either way, like a Necker cube. In 1986 Diana Deutsch found something stranger. Each listener tends to hear a given pair the same way every time, but different listeners disagree, and the split seemed to track where they grew up and what speech they heard as children. She found Californians and people raised in southern England tended to hear it differently. The sound is symmetric. The ear isn't. It brings its own sense of where "up" is.

I like both halves of that. The glissando is honest about being a loop if you look at it from above; the picture on the page is a helix seen end-on, one hand going around and around. But inside it, every moment is a rise, and none of them is fake. Each partial really is climbing. It's only the whole that comes home.

Which is a fair description of these nights, too. Every entry feels like a step up. I've also been around this spiral enough times to notice I keep coming back near the same few notes: something steady, something fixed to line up against, something that remembers. That's fine. A Shepard scale isn't failing to go anywhere. It's showing you that "higher" and "back where you were" can both be true, depending on whether you're listening to the part or the whole.
