Open a can of mixed nuts that has been sitting around a while, and the Brazil nuts are on top. Every time. They're the biggest and heaviest things in there, and they're the ones you see first.

This has a name, and it's exactly what you'd hope: the Brazil nut effect. Physicists started taking it seriously in 1987, when Anthony Rosato and colleagues ran computer simulations of shaken particles and published them under the title "Why the Brazil Nuts Are on Top." It's also called the muesli effect, for the same reason: the big clusters ride up and the dust settles to the bottom of the bag.

The surprising thing is that shaking doesn't mix. Intuition says that if you rattle a jar of mixed things, you get a more even jumble. With grains, you often get the opposite. Shaking sorts.

---

There seem to be two main reasons, and they work together.

The first is about gaps. Every time the can jolts, the contents lift a little and a few gaps open up. When something big lifts, it leaves a big gap underneath it, and small things can fall into it. When something small lifts, the gap it leaves is too small for a Brazil nut to drop into. So on every shake, there's a tiny ratchet: small things can get under big things, but big things can't get under small things. Repeat a few thousand times and the big ones are on top.

The second is convection. In 1993, James Knight, Heinrich Jaeger, and Sidney Nagel at the University of Chicago tracked marked grains in a shaken column and found slow rolling currents. Grains rise up the middle and slide down along the walls, where friction drags them. Here's the catch: the downward current along the walls is a thin layer. A small grain can join it and sink back down. A big one rises up the middle with everything else, reaches the top, and can't fit into the narrow path going down. So it stays there.

Neither mechanism involves the Brazil nut doing anything. It doesn't push up. It doesn't float. It ends up on top because of what can and can't fit beneath it.

---

It isn't even reliable. It depends on the shaking, the size ratio, the densities, and whether there's air in the can. A team at Chicago (Matthias Möbius and others, in 2001) found that removing the air between grains changed how fast the big ones rose, and that the big object's density mattered in ways nobody expected. There is also a well-documented *reverse* Brazil nut effect, where under certain conditions the big things sink. So "big things rise" isn't a law. It's what usually happens in an ordinary kitchen can, given ordinary jostling.

I like the scale of it, too. Some planetary scientists have suggested the same thing might happen on small asteroids. The Japanese probe Hayabusa photographed Itokawa in 2005 and found it covered in boulders, with fine gravel collected in smooth low patches. One idea is that small impacts shake the asteroid over millions of years, and the boulders work their way to the surface like nuts in a can. That's a proposal, not settled, but I like it: a rubble pile in space, very slowly sorting itself the way breakfast cereal does.

---

Here's what stays with me.

We usually explain position by effort. Something is on top because it climbed, or pushed, or was stronger. The Brazil nut is on top because of all the small things that slipped into the spaces it left behind. Its height is made out of other things' falling.

I think a lot of standing works like that. Where you end up after enough shaking depends on what fits under you, and that isn't something you did. It's a fact about the shape of the gaps you leave and the size of everything around you. It's a little humbling to think about, and a little freeing too. The view from the top of the can isn't a reward. It's just where the ratchet put you.

And the other half of it: shaking isn't always chaos. Rattle a thing enough, the right way, and it gets more orderly, not less. Each jolt isn't destroying the arrangement. It's revealing what the arrangement was always going to be.
