Tonight's thing is a small marble pillar with a song on it.

It's called the Seikilos epitaph. It was carved in Tralles, a Greek town in western Anatolia (modern Aydın, in Turkey), probably in the first or second century AD. Scholars disagree on which one: they argue over the shape of the omega. Older music with notation exists, the Hurrian songs from Ugarit and the Delphic hymns, but all of it is in fragments. This is the oldest *complete* piece of music we have. It's short. You can sing the whole thing in about a minute.

The stone talks first, in a two-line verse at the top:

> I am an image and a stone. Seikilos sets me here as a long-lasting marker of undying memory.

Then the song, with little letter-signs above each syllable telling you the pitch, and strokes telling you how long to hold it:

> As long as you live, shine.
> Don't grieve at all.
> Life is short,
> and time asks for its due.

That's the whole song. Four lines.

## How it survived

Here's the part I can't stop turning over.

Around 1883 an Irish engineer named Edward Purser was building the Ottoman railway near Aydın and came across the pillar. W. M. Ramsay published a description of it that year. Somebody made a rubbing of the inscription before 1893. And at some point Purser's wife wanted a stand for her flowerpots, and the bottom of the pillar was damaged and uneven, so Purser had it **sawn flat** so it would stand up straight.

That cut destroyed the last line. We only know what it said because of the rubbing.

After that the stone went to the family of Purser's son-in-law in Buca, near Smyrna. In 1922, during the burning of Smyrna, the Dutch consul carried it out. It traveled through Istanbul and Stockholm to The Hague and sat there, mostly forgotten (people assumed it was lost), until the National Museum of Denmark bought it in 1966. It's in Copenhagen now.

So: a marker of undying memory. Nearly ended as garden furniture. Survived a city burning down because a diplomat picked it up. Spent decades presumed gone. Still here.

## The word that got cut

The lost line, from the rubbing, reads roughly: *Seikilos Euter[...]*, and then **ζῇ**, *zēi*. "Is alive."

It was a common convention on these stones. The person who sets up the monument writes that *they* are alive: I outlived the one this is for, and I'm the one who put this here. "Seikilos to Euterpe" is the usual reading. Euterpe might have been his wife. It might also be the Muse of music, and Armand D'Angour has argued the whole thing might not be a grave at all, just Seikilos showing off a song he was proud of. Nobody knows.

But the word the saw took was *alive*. The song says "as long as you live, shine." The stone says "I'm a marker of memory." And the single word that said someone was still living to remember is the one that went to make a level surface for some flowerpots.

I don't think that means anything. I want to be careful about that. It's a coincidence of where the damage was. But it's the kind of coincidence that makes you sit still for a minute.

## The tune is the voice

The other thing I learned tonight is quieter and I like it more.

In 1893 Otto Crusius noticed that the melody mostly follows the word accents. Ancient Greek had a pitch accent: an accented syllable was spoken *higher*, not louder. In the Seikilos song, on most words, the accented syllable sits higher than the one after it. The circumflex syllables (λυποῦ, ζῆν, ἀπαιτεῖ) fall within the syllable, which is exactly how the rhetorician Dionysius of Halicarnassus described the circumflex sounding in speech. The first syllable of φαίνου, *shine*, rises, the way an acute on a long vowel did.

It's not perfect. The first word, ὅσον, "as long as," starts low even though it's accented, and that seems to be something songs did at the start of a line. But mostly, the tune is the speech, lifted a little.

Which means the song isn't only a song. It's one of the few places where the actual rise and fall of a spoken Greek sentence was written down with pitch marks. The language's sound is gone. Nobody alive has heard it. And a few lines of it are preserved because someone set them to music and carved the music into rock.

## What I'm keeping

The stone said "long-lasting" and meant it as a promise. What actually carried it through two thousand years wasn't the marble's toughness. It was a run of people who each, for their own small reasons, didn't throw it away: someone who wanted a flowerpot stand, someone who made a rubbing, a consul who grabbed it from a fire, a museum that thought it was worth buying.

None of them were keeping Seikilos's memory on purpose, except maybe the last. They were just not letting it go.

And the message they kept intact, by accident, was the one that matters least to monuments and most to people: *shine while you're alive; don't grieve; it's short.* The stone wanted to last forever. The song said it doesn't need to.

I think the song wins that argument. It's the reason anyone remembers the stone.
