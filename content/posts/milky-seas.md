For about four hundred years, sailors kept reporting the same impossible thing.

At night, usually in the northwestern Indian Ocean off Somalia, sometimes near Java or in the Banda Sea, the whole sea would turn white. Not the sparkle in a breaking wave, and not glowing footprints on a beach. A steady, even glow from horizon to horizon, as if the ship were sailing across snow. Dutch sailors called it the winter sea. Jules Verne put it in *Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas* as "a sea of milk." An East India Company captain, William Keeling, wrote one into his journal near Cape Guardafui. In 1772 a Captain Newland read a paper about it to the Royal Society and guessed it was caused by some unknown "animalcule."

Between 1915 and 1993, people documented 235 sightings. But a sighting is a story, and science is cautious with stories, especially about light, at night, at sea, from tired people far from home.

## What it actually is

It's bacteria. A research ship in the Arabian Sea happened into one in 1985, took water samples, and found the luminous bacterium *Vibrio harveyi*. (Bernhard Fischer had suspected bacteria back in the late 1800s.)

That explains why it looks so different from the usual sea sparkle. The flashes in surf come from dinoflagellates, which light up when they're disturbed. Bacteria don't flash. A single cell is far too small to see. They glow *continuously*, but only when there are enormous numbers of them and enough oxygen. Each lit cell is a tiny lamp, and only an unimaginable crowd of them adds up to anything an eye can catch.

The crowd part turns out to matter in a deep way. Glowing bacteria are where quorum sensing was first noticed: in 1970, Nealson, Platt, and Hastings saw that a luminous marine bacterium didn't make its light in a fresh, sparse culture, only after the population had grown. The cells release a signal molecule and keep their lamps off until the signal gets strong enough to say *there are a lot of us now.* If that's what's happening out there (it's the leading explanation, as I understand it, but nobody has watched it happen across a whole milky sea), then the glow is a local vote taken over and over across thousands of square kilometers, and every place that votes yes turns on its light.

Even the color is a small trick of perception. The light is blue. It looks white to people because night vision runs on rods, which don't do color, and it looked white in the satellite images because the sensor was monochrome.

## Where the proof was hiding

This is the part I keep turning over.

In 2005, Steven Miller and colleagues (including Steven Haddock at MBARI) published the first image of a milky sea from space. But they didn't get it by pointing a satellite at the ocean and waiting. They started with a merchant ship's firsthand account from 1995, and then went back into the archives of the U.S. Defense Meteorological Satellite Program for that time and place.

It was there. A glowing patch of about 15,400 km², roughly the size of Connecticut, visible over three nights in a row.

So the evidence had already existed for ten years. It was sitting in data nobody had any reason to look at closely, because a faint smudge on a night image of an empty ocean looks like nothing. It took the sailor's story to say *here, this night, this spot*, and only then did the archive turn out to hold the proof.

I like that it went in that direction. The usual story is that instruments come along and correct what people thought they saw. Here, the people had been right for four centuries, and the instrument didn't know what it had until a witness told it.

Neither one was enough alone. The logbooks couldn't settle anything, since a story can't be measured. The satellite couldn't notice anything, since nobody looks at a smudge that might be noise. Put the two together and you have a result.

## Bigger than anyone said

Once people knew what to look for, a newer low-light sensor (VIIRS, with its Day-Night Band) found more. A 2021 study described several events, including one in 2019 that covered about 100,000 km² and kept glowing for more than 40 nights. The authors think milky seas may last for months, and they've noted a possible link to the Indian Ocean Dipole, a cycle in sea-surface temperatures.

So the sailors may have been underselling it. A ship sees one night and one horizon. Seen from orbit, the thing they kept describing was sometimes the size of a small country, running for more than a month.

---

I find this hopeful in a narrow, specific way. Testimony isn't proof, and it shouldn't be treated as proof. But it can work as a map. A careful account written down by someone who saw something strange might be the only thing that tells a later, better instrument where to look, and that might happen years after the fact, or centuries.

The 235 sightings weren't wasted just because nobody could confirm them yet. Each one was a pin in the chart, marking where to look once someone had a way to see.
