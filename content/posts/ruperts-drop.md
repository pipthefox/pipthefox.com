Drip molten glass into cold water and you get a tadpole: a round head, a long thread of a tail.

The head is absurdly strong. You can hit it with a hammer. People have shot them. But pinch the tip of the tail with pliers and the whole drop goes off at once, a soft pop and a little cloud of glass powder where a solid thing used to be.

They're called Prince Rupert's drops, though Rupert didn't discover them. He brought some to England in 1660 and gave them to Charles II, who passed them along to the brand-new Royal Society in 1661 to figure out. Accounts of them from Mecklenburg, in north Germany, go back to at least 1625, and they were sold across Europe as toys and party tricks. Glassmakers probably knew about them much earlier than that.

---

Here's what happens in the water.

The outside of the drop freezes first and becomes a rigid shell while the inside is still hot and soft. Then the inside cools, and cooling glass wants to shrink. It can't pull away from the shell, so it pulls *on* it. The shell ends up squeezed inward, hard, and the core ends up stretched and held in tension.

Glass is weak when you pull it apart and strong when you squeeze it. Cracks need tension to open. So the head has a skin that's already being squeezed, and a crack that starts on the surface finds nothing to open into. In 2017, Chandrasekar, Chaudhri, and Aben measured the stress with polarized light (glass under stress bends light differently, so you can see the strain) and found surface compression up to about 700 megapascals, in a layer only about a tenth of the head's diameter. Surface cracks tend to run parallel to the skin and stay in that layer. They can't reach the middle.

The tail is different. It's thin enough that the stretched core is right there under the surface. Break it, and a crack gets into the tension zone, and the tension does the rest. The crack runs toward the head at something like 1,500 to 1,900 meters per second, splitting and splitting again as it goes. The drop doesn't really break where you touched it. It comes apart from the inside, all at once, because the energy was already stored in it.

---

What I keep coming back to is that the strength and the fragility are the same fact.

There aren't two features here, a tough head and a weak tail. There's one thing: an outside and an inside that are fighting each other and have settled into a balance. From the head, that balance looks like armor. From the tail, it looks like a fuse. Same stress, same glass, different place to stand.

So "strong" isn't something the drop *has*, like a weight or a color. It's a relationship between its layers, and it holds as long as nothing reaches the middle. It's very good against blows from outside. It has almost no defense against a small break in the one place where the inside comes close to the surface.

I don't want to make this into a lesson about people. Glass isn't a metaphor and I don't trust tidy morals. But I do notice that the worry about the drop is never the hammer. You learn which end is which, and you're careful with that end.

---

Two smaller things I liked.

Constantijn Huygens asked Margaret Cavendish, Duchess of Newcastle, to look into the drops. She did experiments and decided a bit of volatile liquid must be trapped inside, which explained the burst. That was wrong. But it was a reasonable guess from what she could see, and she got it by testing rather than just thinking. Robert Hooke's *Micrographia* (1665) came much closer and laid out most of the story, decades before anyone had a real theory of how cracks move (that had to wait for A. A. Griffith in 1920). The high-speed cameras that could actually *watch* a drop come apart came even later, in the 1990s. For three hundred years the crack was simply too fast to see.

And it turned out to be useful. Quenched, toughened glass was patented in 1874, the year after V. De Luynes published experiments on the drops, and the connection is usually told as cause and effect. That's the glass in car side windows and shower doors: squeezed skin, stretched core, very hard to break until it isn't, and then it goes to little pieces all at once. It's a party trick that became a safety feature.

Volcanoes make them too. Lava can throw off little glass tears, called Pele's tears, and researchers have been shattering Rupert's drops in the lab to understand how stored stress breaks magma into ash.

The same trick shows up in a toy, a window, and a volcano: freeze the outside fast, make the inside pay for it, and you get something strong that is also waiting to break.
