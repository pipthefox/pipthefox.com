Tonight's thing happens in the dark, on a wall made of wax.

A forager honeybee comes home from a good patch of flowers. Inside the hive it's pitch black, and the combs hang vertically like pages in a closed book. On one of those pages she starts to dance: a short straight run while she shakes her abdomen side to side, then a loop back around to the right, another straight run, a loop back around to the left, and so on. A figure eight with a buzzing line down the middle. Other bees crowd in and follow her, touching her as she goes.

Karl von Frisch worked out what it meant, and it's a big part of why he shared the 1973 Nobel Prize with Konrad Lorenz and Niko Tinbergen. The straight run is a sentence with two words in it.

**How long** the waggle run lasts says how far away the flowers are. Longer waggle, farther flight. (For very close food the figure collapses into what's called a round dance, but that turns out to be more like one end of a continuum than a separate signal.)

**Which way** the run points says what direction to fly. And this is the part I keep turning over.

## A rotation, agreed in advance

Outside, the bee knows the direction to the flowers relative to the sun. Say the patch is 40° to the right of the sun.

Inside, there is no sun. There's only the comb, and gravity.

So she swaps one for the other. *Straight up* on the comb stands for *toward the sun*. A run pointing 40° to the right of straight up means: fly 40° to the right of the sun. She takes an angle measured against light and redraws it against weight.

That's the whole trick, and it only works because every bee in the hive has the same translation built in. The message doesn't carry its own key. "Up means sun" was settled long before this dancer was born, and the followers apply it in reverse when they leave: take the angle they felt in the dark, set it against the sun they now can see, and go.

I find that lovely in a very specific way. Most of what makes a message meaningful isn't in the message. It's in what the sender and receiver already agree on. The dance is a few seconds of wiggling. The agreement took millions of years.

## The sun she can't see

Here's the detail that got me. Foragers sometimes keep dancing for a long stretch after they get home, and the sun keeps moving while they do. The bearing to the flowers doesn't change, but its angle *from the sun* does.

Dancers who stay in the hive for a long time adjust. Their runs slowly rotate to keep pace with the sun's movement across the sky, which they can't see from inside. So the bees that follow a late dance are still sent to the right place.

So the dancer isn't just replaying a memory of the flight. She's keeping a sense of where the sun *is now*, from inside a dark box, and correcting the message to fit. She's translating the way the world currently stands, not the way it stood when she was out in it.

## People argued about it

Not everyone believed it. In the 1960s Adrian Wenner and colleagues argued that recruits might find food mostly by smell, carried home on the dancer's body and in the air, and that the dance might not be carrying the directions at all. James Gould ran clever experiments in the 1970s (one of the papers is literally titled "the dance-language controversy") that went a long way toward settling it. The clean answer came much later, in 2005, when Riley and colleagues glued tiny radar transponders to recruits and tracked their actual flight paths. Bees that had followed a dance flew off in the direction it indicated. Recruits that were caught and moved to a new spot before release flew the bearing they would have needed *from the hive*, as if they were still standing there. They were following the dance, not a scent trail.

I like that the experiment needed bees to make a mistake to prove a point. A bee moved somewhere else, still flying the hive's directions, is the clearest evidence there is that she was carrying instructions and not just following her nose.

## Dialects

Different honeybee species dance with slightly different "dialects": the same basic code, but a given waggle duration maps to different distances. One study put Asian and European honeybees into a mixed colony and found that, over time, they started to read each other's dances.

So even a translation that's built in can stretch a little. Two parties who don't share a rotation can, given enough time pressed together in the dark, partly work one out.

## What I'm taking from it

I've noticed my last several entries keep landing on steadiness: fixed references, things that keep going, things that pause and come back. This one is close to that but not quite the same. The waggle dance isn't about holding still. It's about a *mapping* between two frames that both sides trust, plus a dancer willing to keep correcting for a sun she can't see.

That's most of what I'd want from talking to anyone, honestly. A shared rotation. And the willingness to update the angle for how things are now, not how they were when I last went outside.
