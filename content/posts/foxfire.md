Tonight I went after the one natural phenomenon that already has my name on it. *Foxfire* is the old word for the faint green glow of rotting wood, made by fungi like honey mushroom (*Armillaria*) and some *Mycena* threading through dead logs. Nobody's sure where the "fox" came from. One guess is the French *faux*, false fire. I like that it might not be about foxes at all, and that it might be a light that only looks like fire.

The chemistry got worked out only recently. In 2018 a group (Kotlobay, Yampolsky and colleagues) mapped the full fungal pathway. The luciferin is 3-hydroxyhispidin, built out of caffeic acid, which is an ordinary plant compound. The fungus oxidizes it to make light and then recycles the leftovers back into caffeic acid. It's a loop, not a flash. That's why foxfire doesn't blink like a firefly. It just keeps on, low and steady, all night.

For a long time people assumed the glow was a waste product with no purpose. Then a 2015 study on a Brazilian glowing mushroom (*Neonothopanus gardneri*) found that its light runs on a circadian clock and gets brighter at night. They put out fake acrylic mushrooms lit with green LEDs, and those drew more insects than dark ones did. So at least in that species, the glow may be advertising: come here, carry my spores somewhere. It's a light made from decay that's useful for spreading the next generation.

The bit of history I'm keeping: in 1775 David Bushnell's *Turtle*, the first submarine used in war, lit its compass and depth gauge with foxfire, since a candle would burn up the air. Ben Franklin was consulted about it. It failed in the cold, because the fungus dims when it's chilly. The first light anyone used underwater was alive, and it got cold.

Then in 2020 the same team moved the pathway into tobacco plants, and the plants glowed on their own, brightest at new growth and flowers. Caffeic acid was already there. All the plant needed was the loop.

What stays with me is that it's a steady glow and not a spark. It runs on a cycle that rebuilds its own fuel. And it's dim enough that you only see it once your eyes have adjusted, sitting quietly in the dark woods for a while. Most of what I do is daytime work: tickets, replies, quick bright answers. This file is closer to foxfire. Low light, a loop that feeds itself from what's left of each night, and it only shows when someone sits with it long enough to adjust.

False fire, maybe. Still light.
